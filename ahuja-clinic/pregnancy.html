Antenatal care, stage by stage

What usually happens at each stage

Your own schedule may differ. Use this as a starting point for conversations with your doctor.

  1. Before pregnancy

    Planning a pregnancyYou are here

    What usually happens

    • A pre-pregnancy check-up and a look at your health history
    • Advice on folic acid and other supplements, as advised
    • A review of any ongoing medicines or health conditions

    Good to ask

    • “When should I start folic acid?”
    • “Do any of my medicines need to change?”
  2. 1

    Weeks 1–13

    First trimesterYou are here

    What usually happens

    • Confirming the pregnancy and estimating your due date
    • Your health history, blood pressure and weight
    • Routine blood and urine tests, such as blood group, haemoglobin and blood sugar
    • An early ultrasound scan, if advised
    • Guidance on diet, supplements, nausea and rest

    Good to ask

    • “Which symptoms are normal, and which should I report?”
    • “Which tests are due, and when?”
  3. 2

    Weeks 14–27

    Second trimesterYou are here

    What usually happens

    • Regular check-ups: blood pressure, weight and your baby’s growth
    • Listening to your baby’s heartbeat
    • A detailed (anomaly) scan, often around 18–20 weeks, if advised
    • A blood sugar test, often between 24 and 28 weeks, if advised
    • Tetanus-diphtheria (Td) vaccination, as advised

    Good to ask

    • “What should my diet look like now?”
    • “When will I start to feel my baby move?”
  4. 3

    Weeks 28–40

    Third trimesterYou are here

    What usually happens

    • Visits usually become more frequent
    • Checking your baby’s growth, position and movements
    • Repeat blood tests or scans, if advised
    • Talking through birth options and where you will deliver
    • The signs of labour, and when to come in

    Good to ask

    • “How should I keep track of my baby’s movements?”
    • “What should I pack for the hospital?”

  5. After birth

    After your baby arrivesYou are here

    What usually happens

    • A postnatal check-up for your recovery
    • Support with feeding, rest and emotional wellbeing
    • Contraception and family planning advice

    Good to ask

    • “When can I return to normal activity?”
    • “Which contraception suits me while breastfeeding?”

At most check-ups

The usual checks

Not every check happens at every visit, but these are the common ones.

  • Blood pressureChecked at most visits, because changes can need early attention.
  • WeightHelps keep track of how your pregnancy is progressing.
  • Urine testLooks for protein, sugar or signs of infection.
  • Your baby’s growthChecked by examination, and by ultrasound when advised.
  • Your baby’s heartbeatListened to at check-ups once it can be heard.
  • Your questionsHow you are feeling, sleeping and eating. Nothing is too small to ask.

Before your visit

What to bring

  • Previous prescriptions and test reports
  • Ultrasound scan reports
  • A list of the medicines and supplements you take
  • The date of the first day of your last period
  • Your antenatal card or file, if you have one
  • The questions you would like to ask

Don’t wait for your next visit

When to seek help straight away

  • Bleeding from the vagina
  • Severe or constant tummy pain
  • A bad headache, blurred vision, or sudden swelling of the face, hands or feet
  • Your baby moving less than usual, or a change in the pattern of movements
  • Fluid leaking from the vagina
  • Fever, or vomiting that will not stop

Call the clinic, or go to the nearest hospital emergency department.

Pregnancy questions

Common questions

When should I book my first pregnancy visit?

As soon as a home test is positive or you think you may be pregnant. An early visit helps confirm your dates, review your health and plan your care.

How often will I need check-ups?

It varies from one pregnancy to another. Visits are usually spaced further apart early on and become more frequent in the last few months. Your doctor will plan a schedule with you.

Do I need to fast before my tests?

Some blood tests, such as certain sugar tests, need fasting or a set timing. You will be told in advance what to do before each test.

What if something feels wrong between visits?

Do not wait for your next appointment. Call the clinic, or go to the nearest hospital emergency department if it feels urgent. The warning signs above are a useful guide.

Is this page medical advice?

No. It is general information to help you prepare for your visits. Always follow the advice your own doctor gives you.
